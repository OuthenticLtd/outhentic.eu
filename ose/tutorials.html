Tutorial 01 · Practice Module

Slow a song down to learn it.

You found a riff on a video. The fingers fly, the notes blur. You drop the audio into Practice, slow it to 70%, and the pitch stays exactly where it was. Loop the hard bar, drill it, ramp it back up.

Practice Module 4 min · Beginner
SCENE 01 Open the Practice Module

Land on the take you want to study.

From the home grid, tap the Practice tile — the mint-coloured one. The module opens straight on the Library lens because Practice never has the recorder visible; you're here to play along with audio you brought in.

Search by name, narrow by folder, or scroll. Pick the take.

SCENE 02 Tap OPEN to enter the Editor

Practice opens the take fullscreen.

The mini-visualiser at the top of the Library has an OPEN pill in mint. Tap it — or tap anywhere on the waveform thumbnail — and the whole screen lifts into the Audio Editor with the take loaded and the playhead pinned at the centre.

Same editor the Audio Module uses. Same engine. Different framing: in Practice you're not editing the take, you're studying it.

SCENE 03 Open the SPEED & PITCH fold

The fold strip is at the bottom of the editor.

Five icon-only tabs: EDIT · SPEED & PITCH · LOOPS · REPAIR · DSP. Tap the speed-gauge icon. The fold expands above the canvas with a semitone wheel, a cents slider, and a speed wheel.

The wheel is the one you want. It's connected to a phase-locked phase-vocoder under the hood — what that means in practice: you can change time without changing pitch, and vice versa. They're decoupled.

SCENE 04 Drag the speed wheel

The waveform compresses. The pitch holds.

Drag the wheel down toward 50%. The waveform on the canvas above visibly stretches — same audio, longer in time. Hit play and listen: the pitch is exactly where it was. Notes hold their place, transients stay sharp.

This isn't a slow-down with the chipmunk effect. It's the same engine the Audio Editor uses everywhere else. Quality is the same whether you're stretching by 5% or by 50%.

SCENE 05 Loop the hard bar

Drag a region. Promote it to a loop.

Toggle the Select tool from the editor's toolbar (the rectangle icon). Drag a rectangle around the bar that's giving you trouble. Tap the loop verb. The selection promotes to a loop region — sky-blue pillars at each end, sample-perfect when it wraps.

Now play. The two-bar passage drills itself for you while you focus on the fingers.

Pro tips for Practice

  • The phase-locked stretch lets you change pitch and time independently. Drop the speed to 70% AND transpose down a semitone if the song is in the wrong key for your voice.
  • Marker pins work great in Practice. Drop a marker at the start of every difficult passage so you can jump between them with the −5s / +5s chrome bar buttons.
  • Bind your foot pedal to the loop's start/stop via MIDI Learn (long-press). Hands-free practice is the dream.
  • The Practice library shares takes with the Audio module's library. Recordings made anywhere in OSE show up here automatically.
Tutorial 02 · Play Module

Layer two voices in one preset.

Solo piano on the bottom half of the keyboard. Strings join from middle C up. Be the whole band — left hand bass, right hand pad, all from one preset. The keyboard's tinted in stripes so you can see exactly which voice you're triggering.

Play Module 5 min · Intermediate
SCENE 01 Open the Instrument Editor

Tap the pencil in the top bar.

From the Play Module, the four chrome icons in the top bar are: metronome, edit pencil, sessions and record. Tap the pencil. It tilts and dips slightly — the writing-posture animation — then the Instrument Editor dialog opens.

Inside, you'll see the current instrument as a single layer. We're about to add a second.

SCENE 02 Add the second layer

Pick the strings.

Tap ＋ Add Layer. The Instrument Source picker opens — a chip strip across the top (Favourites, OSE built-ins, Imported SoundFonts, User Presets, Multi-Presets), a search field, and a vertical grid of folders and instrument cards. Each card carries the instrument's skin colour.

Find your strings preset. Tap it. The new layer drops into the editor with sky-blue accent.

SCENE 03 Set the key range

Strings start at middle C.

In the Layer 2 row, tap the key-range field. Two thumb pillars appear on a tiny piano strip. Drag the left thumb up to C4. Now the strings only sound from C4 upward — below that, you only hear the piano.

Each layer carries its own key range, instrument source, skin colour, microtonal scale and display name.

SCENE 04 Play. The keyboard tells you which voice you're on.

Two-colour stripes mean two voices.

Close the editor. Look at the keyboard. The bottom half is tinted in the piano's accent. The top half is tinted blue for the strings. Where the two zones overlap (if you set them to), you'd see stripes of both colours on the same key.

Pull a chord. Left hand under C4 — piano. Right hand above — strings. Both, played together, become the texture of the song.

Pro tips for layered presets

  • Multi-instrument zones can OVERLAP. Set Layer 1's range to A0–C8 and Layer 2's range to C4–C8. Below C4 you only hear Layer 1; above C4 you hear both, layered.
  • Each zone has its own skin colour (12 user-selectable skins). Pick contrasting colours so your eyes can separate the zones at a glance on a busy keyboard.
  • Each zone can also carry its own microtonal scale via the small scale micro-button on the right edge of the zone header. Useful for sitar over a Western lead.
  • Built-in OSE instruments are read-only. The save button forces \"Save as New Preset\" — your edit becomes its own thing in the User Presets folder.
  • Drum-pad mode is a layout toggle on the preset, not a separate module. The same multi-zone concept becomes 16 colour-coded pads.
Tutorial 03 · Tuner

Tune to ±3 cents and let the snap-lock confirm.

Two minutes before downbeat. The bass is flat and there's no time. Open the Tuner. The vintage half-arc dial wakes up. Pluck — needle drifts. You tighten — needle approaches centre. Inside ±3 cents the whole thing snaps green and the LED haloes light up. You're done. You go back to the song.

Tuner 2 min · Beginner
SCENE 01 Open the Tuner

Home grid → TUNER tile.

The Tuner's accent is sky blue. The screen wakes up with a vintage PTU-2-style meter face — warm amber glass, half-arc dial, ghost note label, 32-band live spectrum ribbon below the dial. Below the dial sits a 3-LED tuning strip: red flat / green in-tune / red sharp.

If you've never granted microphone permission, OSE asks now. The tuner doesn't store anything — the audio goes straight to the YIN pitch detector and never touches disk.

SCENE 02 Play a note. Watch the needle drift.

Around ±10 cents, the needle is alive.

Pluck the string. The note name lights up — let's say A3 — and the needle starts moving. If you're flat, the needle sits left of centre. Sharp, right of centre. The cents readout under the dial shows you exactly: −12¢, +8¢, etc.

The 32 bands of the live spectrum ribbon under the dial show your harmonic content — useful for stringed instruments where you want to see overtones settle.

SCENE 03 Tighten until the needle locks

Inside ±3 cents, OSE makes a decision.

The just-noticeable-difference for pitch is around 5–6 cents. The 3-cent band sits comfortably inside that. So when you cross into ±3 cents, the needle snaps to centre, the cents readout paints LED green, and the LED haloes around the dial light up.

A 1-cent flutter doesn't pull the needle around — the lock is forgiving on purpose. Your eyes get a clear "yes, you're there" instead of fighting a forever-twitching reading.

Pro tips for the Tuner

  • The reference tone runs at the device's native output sample rate. Cheap converters often resample at 44.1k or 48k regardless of source — OSE bypasses that so the reference tone stays exactly on pitch.
  • The tuning fork button gives you a percussive ADSR pluck. The speaker button gives you a sustained sine. Use the fork to match by ear, the speaker to hold a note for a full bar's worth of tuning.
  • Switch input device or USB channel mid-session — the engine restarts cleanly without you needing to leave the module.
  • Settings → Notation lets you swap between English-Sharp / English-Flat / German / Solfège. Settings → Temperament covers Equal / Just / Pythagorean. Settings → Transposition handles transposing instruments (Bb trumpet, etc.).
  • Snap-in-tune is UI-only — the engine still publishes the raw cents value. Toggle it off in Settings if you'd rather see every micro-flutter.
Tutorial 04 · Audio Module

Capture an idea between sets.

Set's over. You're tearing down. An idea hits — a hook, a chord, a melody you'll forget in two minutes. Tap AUDIO. The module opens straight on the recorder lens because that's where you usually want to be when you tap AUDIO. Tap REC. Hum it, sing it, play it. Tap STOP. It's saved. The full editor is waiting for you when you get home.

Audio Module 3 min · Beginner
SCENE 01 Decide MONITOR / PRINT before you record

Both default to OFF. That's deliberate.

OFF on MONITOR means you don't hear yourself. OFF on PRINT means the DSP chain is bypassed in the file — you get the raw input. That's the safest default for capturing an idea fast: silent, dry, fast.

Turn MONITOR on if you want to hear yourself. Turn PRINT on if you want the EQ / compression / reverb you've dialled in to bake into the file. They're independent — any combination is valid.

SCENE 02 Hit the REC pill

Pre-roll counts down if you set one.

The 64dp red REC circle is at the bottom of the recorder lens, with a 36dp safe-area cushion. If you've set pre-roll to 3, 5 or 10 seconds in Settings, a giant accent number overlays the screen counting down. Tap during pre-roll to cancel.

Once it's running, the timer turns red, the visualiser switches to live waveform mode, and new peak buckets grow leftward past the centred playhead.

SCENE 03 Stop, the take auto-selects

Go listen. Edit later if you want.

Tap STOP. The take publishes and immediately auto-selects in the visualiser at the top so you can audition it. Flip to LIBRARY and the new take is at the top of the list. Tap it later from anywhere — the Audio Editor opens fullscreen, your edits are non-destructive, you can Bounce or Revert at any time.

If you turned PRINT on, the offline DSP bake fires when you leave the lens or start a new take. PRINT bakes on leave, not on stop — stopping is for staying and listening to what you just played.

Pro tips for fast capture

  • The library lens has a floating REC FAB at bottom-right. One tap arms recording from anywhere — no need to flip lenses first.
  • Settings → Recording Settings has a "Block calls during recording" switch that pushes the device into Do-Not-Disturb (alarms only) for the duration of the take. No more ruined captures because someone called.
  • Built-in microphones collapse to a single "Internal device" row in the input picker. Plug in a USB interface and every channel appears.
  • Effect tail length (OFF / AUTO / 1s … 15s) controls how much reverb/delay decay gets captured after you stop. AUTO ends when the tail decays to silence.
  • Pair MONITOR and PRINT off to capture silently. Both on, you hear yourself and bake the chain. Either-or is fine too — it's a matrix, not a single switch.
Tutorial 05 · Metronome

A 5/4 metronome with a polygon you can feel.

You're working on a 5/4 fill. The polygon at the centre of the metronome's hero face is a pentagon — five sides, one per beat. The bright dot orbits its perimeter and lands on each vertex precisely on the beat. Beat 1 is accented; you feel exactly which side of the bar you're on at a glance.

Metronome 3 min · Beginner
SCENE 01 Set the BPM

Drag the scrubber, or tap to numeric-edit.

The hero is the ClickTrackFace: a 220–280dp tall amber-glass card with a 50sp light-weight monospace BPM digit and the word ALLEGRO (or whatever marking matches your tempo) under it. Drag the BPM scrubber below the hero or tap the digit to inline-edit with a numeric keyboard.

Tempo markings update live: under 60 = LARGO, under 76 = ADAGIO, under 108 = ANDANTE, under 120 = MODERATO, under 156 = ALLEGRO, etc.

SCENE 02 Set the time signature

Watch the polygon morph.

Below the hero, the BEATS stepper changes the numerator. Tap + and the polygon at the centre of the hero gains a side. Three beats = triangle. Four = square. Five = pentagon. Six = hexagon. The shape is the time signature.

The denominator (the bottom number) is a separate button. Common settings: 3/4, 4/4, 5/4, 6/8, 7/8, 5/8.

SCENE 03 Tap beat numerals to cycle accents

Beat 1 accented. Beat 3 silent. Beat 5 ghosted.

Below the hero, a strip of beat numerals (1 through your numerator) shows each beat. Tap any beat to cycle its accent state: silent → normal → accented. The active beat lights up while the click runs. Tap START. The polygon's orbit dot lands on each vertex on the beat; the click fires; you can hear which side of the bar you're on with your eyes shut.

Pro tips for the metronome

  • The polygon is the time signature. No /4 readout needed in chrome. Once you've got the connection, you can sight-read odd-meter pieces faster because the visual matches the physical bar.
  • Tempo word lookup: under 60 = LARGO, 60–76 = ADAGIO, 76–108 = ANDANTE, 108–120 = MODERATO, 120–156 = ALLEGRO, 156–168 = VIVACE, 168–200 = PRESTO, above = PRESTISSIMO.
  • Bind the TAP button via MIDI Learn (long-press) so a foot pedal sets tempo on stage. Two consecutive taps and you've got it.
  • The metronome is also reachable from the Play Module via the metronome chip in the top bar — same engine, same presets, same accents. Change tempo here and the Play Module follows.
  • The orbit dot's cadence tells you immediately when the click is fighting the bar. If the dot lands between vertices, your time signature doesn't match the music.
Tutorial 06 · Live

Bind a foot pedal with MIDI Learn.

You're playing live. The hands are full. You don't want to lift one off the keys to flip the loop on the second chorus. Long-press the loop button. The MIDI Learn sheet slides up. Tap your pedal. Done. The pedal is now the loop button. Same trick for transport, sustain, the modulation wheel — anything in OSE responds to it.

Live · system-wide 3 min · Beginner
SCENE 01 Long-press anything you can touch

Knob, slider, pill, transport — they all respond.

Long-press is reserved app-wide for MIDI Learn. There's no other gesture that triggers it, so you don't have to think about whether this button supports it — they all do. The chevrons next to the instrument name. The sustain pedal. The Cut/Copy/Paste verbs. The metronome chip. The PLAY/STOP transport. Anything.

SCENE 02 The MIDI Learn sheet slides up

Press a key, turn a knob, tap a pedal.

The sheet shows you what's listening. Hit the hardware control you want bound. The next CC, note or program-change message OSE receives gets mapped to the long-pressed control. Bindings are global and channel-aware. The sheet shows the binding that was just made and dismisses itself.

Bindings live in the Settings → MIDI Mappings category. You can review, clear, or relearn any of them at any time.

Pro tips for live performance

  • Bindings are global and channel-aware. The same CC can hit different controls when the channel changes, so a multi-instrument setup can use one MIDI controller across many duties.
  • Most useful bindings: a foot pedal to sustain (CC 64 by default), a foot pedal to loop start/stop, a knob to filter cutoff, a fader to expression, the modulation wheel to LFO depth. None of these need a special config — just long-press and assign.
  • Bluetooth MIDI keyboards work the same way. Pair via Settings → Bluetooth MIDI Devices and any binding you make from a Bluetooth controller is identical to a wired one.
  • USB MIDI is plug-and-play. The engine recovers automatically through Android's hot-plug callbacks if a cable disconnects.
  • Bindings persist between sessions. Set them once during soundcheck and they're there next gig.
